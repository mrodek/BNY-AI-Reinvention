# AI-Native Enterprise Workflows

Some of the most valuable early AI-native use cases are the ones clients experience as journeys rather than products. The hard part is not a single task inside a single business line. The hard part is getting identity, documents, entitlements, approvals, communications, and evidence to move together without dropping context along the way. That is where an enterprise workflow becomes visible, and where the value of a shared operating platform stops being abstract.^[SRC-001]

These workflows matter because they concentrate friction. A client does not experience a company as a set of org charts. The client experiences a stalled onboarding, a missing signer verification, a vote approaching deadline, or a request to resubmit information the institution should already know. When BNY talks publicly about quicker onboarding, coordinated delivery, unified platforms, and AI embedded into workflows, it is pointing toward exactly this category of problem.^[SRC-001]

## What Makes These Workflows Different

An enterprise workflow is one whose outcome depends on shared state across multiple domains. The institution has to know which legal entity it is dealing with, what authority that entity has granted, what documents already exist, what deadlines apply, what products or accounts are involved, and what actions have already been taken. If those facts are fragmented, the workflow slows down even when each local team is doing its job.

That is why shallow automation is not enough. A copilot can summarize an email trail. It cannot by itself decide whether a legal entity is ready to activate, whether the correct signer is acting on its behalf, whether an investor instruction conflicts with standing policy, or whether a vote was processed and evidenced correctly. These are stateful workflows with control boundaries, time pressure, and audit consequences.

## Post-KYC Onboarding Is Where Time To Revenue Gets Lost

Post-KYC onboarding is a good example because the risk review may be substantially complete while the client is still far from operational. After due diligence clears, the institution still has to assemble tax forms, account structures, settlement instructions, signer authority, channel entitlements, product elections, document packages, and activation steps across multiple teams and systems.

The industry data shows how expensive that gap can be. McKinsey reports that onboarding a new corporate client can take up to 100 days, and that more than 40 percent of the timeline can be consumed by KYC due diligence and account opening.^[SRC-010] That means a large share of the delay sits in the work surrounding the decision, not only in the decision itself. GLEIF's research also found average onboarding times of roughly six weeks in its surveyed sample and documented the frequent use of multiple identifiers for the same organization.^[SRC-007] In practice, the institution is often fighting fragmented identity, fragmented documents, and fragmented authority long after the formal risk gate has been passed.

BNY already has public workflow signals that make this concrete. Pershing describes a single account-opening platform that pre-populates applications, supports multiple account openings, gathers secure e-signatures, generates forms, and preserves full audit details.^[SRC-002] That is important not because it is a prettier front end, but because it shows the right operating shape: one workflow carrying context forward instead of restarting from scratch at each handoff.

The economic case is strong. McKinsey says straight-through KYC and related operating-model improvements can reduce operating costs by 20 to 30 percent, improve quality by 15 to 40 percent, and in some cases cut low-risk review time from about 100 minutes to 30 minutes.^[SRC-009] McKinsey also argues that better onboarding improves EBITDA while reducing onboarding cost.^[SRC-010] Those are not BNY-specific promises, but they support a disciplined inference: if post-KYC onboarding is redesigned as a continuous workflow rather than a chain of queues, the benefits should come from three places. The first is lower operations cost because fewer cases are reopened and fewer documents are reworked. The second is earlier revenue activation because accounts and services go live faster. The third is better conversion into additional services because the client is not lost in the handoff between risk completion and operational readiness.

An AI-native version of the workflow starts with context assembly. The system should know which entity has passed due diligence, which accounts are being opened, which forms can be generated or reused, which signer has the necessary role, which downstream teams still need to act, and where the case is blocked. Swift's KYC Registry points in this direction with standardized KYC data, reusable documentation, audit trails, access controls, and API-based updates.^[SRC-006] GLEIF's vLEI work points in the same direction for machine-verifiable role authority.^[SRC-008]

The practical result is not full autonomy. It is continuity. The platform can preassemble the case, generate the next valid package, surface missing items before they become a delay, and route the work with a visible reason for every step. The client sees a cleaner path to activation. The operator sees a smaller exception set. Management sees where the workflow is actually leaking time and labor.

## Proxy Voting Is A Real Business Workflow, Not A Governance Sideshow

Proxy voting deserves a clearer business definition than it usually gets. At its simplest, proxy voting is the process by which shareholders vote on matters such as director elections, executive compensation, corporate actions, and shareholder proposals without attending the meeting in person.^[SRC-011] Operationally, though, it is much more than vote capture. The workflow runs from meeting-event intake through position and entitlement determination, policy interpretation, instruction capture, deadline management, vote submission, reconciliation, confirmation, disclosure, and reporting.^[SRC-004]^[SRC-005]

That matters to customers because voting is one of the most direct expressions of ownership. Asset managers, institutions, intermediaries, and underlying investors care about whether voting rights are exercised accurately, on time, and in a way that matches policy or client preference. As pass-through voting expands, the workflow matters even more because investors increasingly expect more choice, more transparency, and better proof that their instructions were represented correctly.^[SRC-012]

It matters to BNY because this workflow sits naturally at the intersection of records, entitlements, client servicing, deadlines, policy control, and evidence. BNY's customers do not experience proxy voting as a narrow back-office utility. They experience it as part of the trust and operating quality of the platform handling their assets, accounts, and instructions. If the workflow is hard to navigate, hard to evidence, or slow to reconcile, the institution looks fragmented precisely where stewardship and control matter most.

The scale is large enough to justify treating it as a lighthouse workflow. Broadridge says more than 16.3 billion shares were voted through ProxyVote in 2023, representing 22 percent of total voted shares.^[SRC-011] It also says pass-through voting capabilities are available across more than 600 funds and over $8 trillion in assets.^[SRC-012] Its vote-confirmation offering spans more than 2,000 U.S. public companies.^[SRC-013] Those figures do not measure BNY's economics directly, but they do establish that this is not a niche administrative process. It is a high-volume, evidence-sensitive workflow with growing expectations around choice and traceability.

The economic upside follows from that operating reality. Better workflow design should reduce manual exception handling, lower reconciliation effort, and shrink the cost of deadline-driven outreach and rework. It should also increase client value where investors care about policy transparency, confirmation, reporting quality, and pass-through choice. That value may show up as lower servicing cost in some segments and as better retention, wallet share, or asset capture in others. The key point is that the economics come from both efficiency and trust.

The control logic is especially important here. The SEC makes clear that advisers with proxy-voting authority must maintain policies and procedures and disclose how clients can obtain voting information and understand those procedures.^[SRC-004] So the target state is not a bot that votes on behalf of everyone. It is a supervised system that assembles the right facts, applies the right policy, identifies where client outreach is required, routes clean cases efficiently, and preserves a recoverable evidence trail for exceptions, overrides, and confirmation.

## What The Platform Changes

Both workflows need the same underlying capabilities even though they look different on the surface.

Canonical data products come first. Onboarding depends on authoritative views of the client, entity, account, signer, document, and product state. Proxy voting depends on authoritative views of positions, entitlements, meeting events, policies, instructions, and submission status. Without that substrate, the institution is asking people to bridge gaps manually.

The control plane comes next. These workflows cannot tolerate ambiguous authority. The system has to know who may view a document package, request additional forms, change a standing instruction, release a ballot, or finalize a sensitive activation step. Shared controls are not background plumbing here. They are part of the business workflow itself.

The runtime is what turns context into action. In onboarding, it gathers forms, checks completeness, triggers signature requests, routes approvals, and escalates broken chains. In proxy voting, it assembles holdings and events, applies policies, prepares outreach, monitors deadlines, and keeps the case recoverable when exceptions appear. Observability then closes the loop. Repeated delays in signer verification, repeated overrides in one voting category, or repeated document reopen rates for one client segment are not isolated annoyances. They are design signals.

## Where Autonomy Helps, And Where It Should Stop

The strongest early autonomy lies in retrieval, validation, case assembly, deadline monitoring, document matching, suggested routing, and explanation. Those are the steps where the machine can remove drag without taking consequential decisions out of governed human hands.

The tighter human boundary should stay around attestations, sensitive account activation, disputed authority, policy overrides, and final disclosure-sensitive releases. The goal is not zero-touch operations everywhere. The goal is fewer avoidable handoffs, better prepared cases, faster clean-path processing, and better evidence when judgment still matters.

These workflows are useful precisely because they make the enterprise value proposition concrete. They show how shared context, shared controls, and coordinated execution can improve client experience and lower operational drag at the same time. They also show why the next strategic question is not only how each business uses AI. It is how the institution represents the relationships that let work move cleanly across businesses in the first place.
