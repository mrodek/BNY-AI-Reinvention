# Appendix B: Nonstandard Collateral

## Purpose

This appendix provides context for a question that sits adjacent to CH10 but should not dominate it: what changes when the collateral is not a standard marketable financial asset.

CH10 is centered on securities-based collateral and secured-financing workflows. The core operating model there is built around standardized eligibility schedules, pricing references, haircuts, margining, substitutions, settlement timing, and collateral mobility across established market infrastructure. That world is still operationally complex, but it is complex inside a relatively standardized system.

Nonstandard collateral is different.

## What Counts As Nonstandard Collateral Here

For the purposes of this appendix, nonstandard collateral means assets whose valuation, liquidity, transferability, legal enforceability, or monitoring requirements are materially less standardized than Treasury securities, listed equities, cash, or other widely accepted financial collateral. Depending on the lending context, this can include assets such as fine art, farmland, private business interests, specialized real estate, or other bespoke hard-to-price holdings.

The exact boundary depends on the lending and legal structure. The point of the category is not that these assets are impossible to finance. It is that they do not fit cleanly into the highly standardized collateral operations described in CH10.

## Why The Workflow Gets More Manual

The first reason is valuation. Standard financial collateral usually has a visible market price, accepted market conventions, and infrastructure that supports daily mark-to-market logic. Nonstandard collateral often depends on appraisals, infrequent comparable transactions, asset-specific assumptions, and a higher degree of judgment. That makes haircut policy, revaluation cadence, and dispute resolution less mechanical.

The second reason is liquidity. Standard financial collateral can often be substituted, financed, or liquidated through well-understood market channels. Nonstandard collateral is usually less liquid, more expensive to move, and harder to turn into cash quickly without value loss. That changes both risk appetite and operational response under stress.

The third reason is control and enforceability. In securities-based collateral workflows, custody, perfection, settlement, and transfer are supported by market infrastructure and common operating patterns. With bespoke assets, control rights, liens, title verification, documentation quality, custody arrangements, insurance, and liquidation procedures may vary much more from case to case.

## Why This Matters For The Book

This contrast helps sharpen the scope of CH10. The main chapter is not about every asset that can theoretically be pledged. It is about the current financial-market operating system for collateral, securities lending, repo, and financing. In that system, the core burden is orchestration across standardized but still fragmented workflows.

Nonstandard collateral matters because it shows the limit of that standardization. As assets become more bespoke, the workflow tends to become more human, more judgment-heavy, and more dependent on case-by-case controls. That is useful context, but it is not the main operating center of the BNY role described in CH10 based on the current source set.

## Scope Guardrail

This appendix should not be read as a claim that nonstandard collateral is a core part of BNY's current collateral-management platform role in the way repo, securities lending, triparty, Treasury financing, and derivatives-margin support are. Based on the current evidence base for CH10, the chapter should stay centered on standard financial collateral and secured-financing workflows unless later research establishes a stronger reason to broaden that scope.
